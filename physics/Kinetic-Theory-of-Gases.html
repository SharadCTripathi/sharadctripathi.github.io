Kinetic Theory of Gases and Its Applications

Dr. Sharad Chandra Tripathi

What Is the Kinetic Theory of Gases?

Let’s explore what happens inside a gas at the molecular level. The Kinetic Theory of Gases helps us understand how a huge number of tiny particles, molecules or atoms, zoom around randomly, bump into each other, and hit the container walls. All of this chaotic motion, surprisingly, gives rise to very predictable things like pressure and temperature.

This theory acts as a bridge between the microscopic world (molecular motion) and the macroscopic quantities we measure in labs—pressure, volume, temperature, and so on.

Here are the main assumptions this theory makes:

  • Gases consist of a large number of extremely small molecules.

  • These molecules are always moving in random, straight-line paths until they collide.

  • All collisions—whether between molecules or with container walls—are perfectly elastic (no energy is lost).

  • The actual volume of gas molecules is negligible compared to the space they occupy.

  • Except during collisions, no attractive or repulsive forces act between molecules.

Of course, these assumptions are idealizations. Real gases deviate from them under high pressure or low temperature, but for most practical purposes, especially at room temperature and atmospheric pressure, this model works beautifully.

Key Concepts and Definitions

Now let’s break down the most important ideas one by one:

  • Pressure of a Gas: Caused by molecules colliding with container walls. \[P = \frac{1}{3} \rho \bar{c}^2\] where \(\rho\) is the mass density and \(\bar{c}\) is the root mean square speed. This connects random molecular motion to observable pressure.

  • Root Mean Square Speed (rms speed): \[c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}\] Here, \(k\) is the Boltzmann constant, \(T\) is temperature, \(R\) is the gas constant, and \(M\) is molar mass. This gives us a measure of how fast the molecules are moving on average.

  • Molecular Speed Distribution: Not all molecules move at the same speed. In fact, their speeds follow a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. At any temperature, some molecules are slow, some are fast, and most are somewhere in the middle. That’s why we use different kinds of averages, like most probable speed, average speed, and rms speed, to describe them.

  • Collision Frequency (\(z\)): This tells us how often a molecule collides with others in one second. \[z \propto n \bar{c}\] where \(n\) is number density and \(\bar{c}\) is average speed. As you might guess, more molecules or faster speeds means more frequent collisions.

  • Mean Free Path (\(\lambda\)): The average distance a molecule travels before bumping into another. A high-pressure gas has a shorter mean free path due to more frequent collisions.

  • Kinetic Energy of One Molecule: \[KE = \frac{3}{2}kT\] Interesting fact: this depends only on temperature—not mass or speed.

  • Kinetic Energy of One Mole of Gas: \[KE = \frac{3}{2}RT\] This shows that 1 mole of any ideal gas has the same total kinetic energy at a given temperature.

  • Ideal Gas Equation: \[PV = nRT\] This is the famous equation that connects pressure (P), volume (V), temperature (T), and the amount of gas (n). Derived using kinetic theory and experimental results.

  • Degrees of Freedom: These refer to the independent ways a molecule can store energy—translational, rotational, and vibrational. Monoatomic gases like helium only have 3 (just moving in 3D space), but diatomic gases like O2 can rotate and eventually vibrate too (at higher temperatures), giving them 5 or even more degrees of freedom.

Shortcut Concepts

Let’s simplify even further:

  • Gas pressure = molecules hitting walls.

  • Temperature = average kinetic energy.

  • \(KE_{\text{avg}} \propto T\), so hotter gas = faster molecules.

  • Lighter gases = higher speed at the same \(T\).

  • Specific heat is tied to degrees of freedom (via the equipartition theorem).

Examples

Let’s solve a few real problems to lock in the ideas.

  1. Calculate rms speed: Find the rms speed of oxygen molecules at \(300\,\text{K}\). Molar mass \(M = 32 \,\text{g/mol}\).

    First, convert molar mass to kg/mol: \(M = 0.032\,\text{kg/mol}\)

    \[c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 \cdot 8.314 \cdot 300}{0.032}} \approx 483\,\text{m/s}\]

  2. Find pressure from rms speed: A gas with density \(\rho = 1.5\,\text{kg/m}^3\) has \(c_{\text{rms}} = 500\,\text{m/s}\). What’s the pressure?

    \[P = \frac{1}{3} \rho c_{\text{rms}}^2 = \frac{1}{3} \cdot 1.5 \cdot (500)^2 = 125,000\,\text{Pa}\]

  3. Average Kinetic Energy: What is the average KE of a gas molecule at \(T = 300\,\text{K}\)?

    \[KE = \frac{3}{2}kT = \frac{3}{2} \cdot 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \cdot 300 \approx 6.21 \times 10^{-21}\,\text{J}\]

Concept Questions with Explanations

Here are some quick questions that often confuse students—but won’t confuse you anymore:

  1. What causes gas pressure?
    It’s just billions of molecules slamming into container walls every second. Each tiny hit adds up to measurable pressure.

  2. Why is temperature linked to molecular energy?
    Because temperature is literally a measure of average kinetic energy. If temperature goes up, molecules move faster.

  3. Do all gas molecules in a sample move at the same speed?
    Not at all. Speeds vary and follow the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. That’s why we talk about rms speed and average speed.

  4. What is the mean free path?
    It’s the average distance a molecule travels before a collision. Higher pressure or denser gases = shorter mean free path.

  5. What changes if a gas is compressed?
    Molecules are packed closer, so collisions happen more often. Pressure rises, mean free path decreases, and if done quickly, temperature may increase too (adiabatic compression).

Super Tips for Solving Fast

Here are some rapid tricks to help you ace problems:

  • For rms speed: \[c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} \quad \text{(make sure } M \text{ is in kg/mol)}\]

  • Given density and speed? Use: \[P = \frac{1}{3} \rho \bar{c}^2\]

  • Average molecular energy: \[KE_{\text{avg}} = \frac{3}{2}kT\]

  • SI Units reminder: \[R = 8.314\,\text{J/mol·K}, \quad k = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\,\text{J/K}\]

  • Gas law conversions: \[PV = nRT, \quad \frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2} \quad \text{(when } n \text{ is constant)}\]

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Q1. The number of air molecules per cm\(^3\) increased from \(3 \times 10^{19}\) to \(12 \times 10^{19}\). The ratio of collision frequency of air molecules before and after the increase in number respectively is:
[JEE, 2023]

  1. 0.75

  2. 0.50

  3. 1.25

  4. 0.25

Solution:

Collision frequency \(z \propto n\), where \(n\) is the number density of molecules.
So, if \(n\) increases from \(3 \times 10^{19}\) to \(12 \times 10^{19}\), the ratio of initial to final collision frequency is: \[\frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{3 \times 10^{19}}{12 \times 10^{19}} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25\]

Answer: \(\boxed{0.25}\)
