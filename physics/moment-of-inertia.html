Moment of Inertia

Imagine you are trying to spin a big wheel. Sometimes it is easy to spin, and sometimes it is hard. Why does this happen?

This is because of something called moment of inertia. It helps us understand how much an object resists spinning.

Moment of inertia is like “mass” but for spinning things. Just like heavy things are hard to move, things with a big moment of inertia are hard to spin or stop spinning.

Here are some important points to remember:

  • Moment of inertia tells us how hard it is to start or stop spinning.
  • It depends on two things: how heavy the object is, and how far the weight is from the center of spinning (called the axis).
  • If the weight is far from the axis, the object is harder to spin.
  • If the weight is close to the axis, the object is easier to spin.

Think about opening a door. If you push near the hinge (the axis), it is hard to open. But if you push at the edge of the door (far from the hinge), it opens easily. This happens because pushing far from the axis gives more turning power.

In the same way, objects with weight far from their center resist spinning more. This means they have a bigger moment of inertia.

So, moment of inertia is how much something resists spinning. It depends on how heavy it is and where its weight is placed.

How Is Moment of Inertia Calculated?

Let's break it down clearly:

  • For a single point mass:
    • \[ I = m r^2 \]

    where \( m \) is the mass and \( r \) is the distance from the axis of rotation.

  • For an extended object, split it into tiny mass elements and sum them:
    • \[ I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2 \]

    or, in the continuous case using calculus,

    \[ I = \int r^2 \, dm \]

This integral adds up all the tiny contributions to the total resistance to spinning.

Why Should You Care?

  • Engine flywheels: These help maintain smooth engine performance by resisting sudden changes in rotational speed. Their large moment of inertia stores rotational energy and prevents stalling.
  • Figure skaters: When skaters pull in their arms, they spin faster. This is because reducing their moment of inertia increases angular velocity to conserve angular momentum.
  • Bicycle wheels: Larger wheels have more moment of inertia, helping maintain forward motion. That’s why once you're rolling, staying upright becomes easier.
  • Industrial machines: From turbines to washing machines, engineers adjust the moment of inertia to control torque, acceleration, and energy efficiency.

It’s also a key player in Newton’s second law for rotation:

\[ \tau = I \alpha \]

where \( \tau \) is torque, \( I \) is moment of inertia, and \( \alpha \) is angular acceleration. Just like \( F = ma \) governs linear motion, this governs how rotational speed changes under applied torque.

Common Shapes and Their Moments of Inertia

  • Thin ring about its central axis:
    • \[ I = MR^2 \]
  • Disc or solid cylinder through center:
    • \[ I = \tfrac{1}{2} MR^2 \]
  • Thin rod:
    • About center: \( I = \tfrac{1}{12} ML^2 \)
    • About one end: \( I = \tfrac{1}{3} ML^2 \)
  • Solid sphere through its diameter:
    • \[ I = \tfrac{2}{5} MR^2 \]
  • Hollow sphere through its diameter:
    • \[ I = \tfrac{2}{3} MR^2 \]

Useful Theorems

  • Parallel Axis Theorem
    • \[ I = I_{\text{CM}} + M d^2 \]
  • Perpendicular Axis Theorem
    • \[ I_z = I_x + I_y \]

Worked Example

\[ I = \tfrac{1}{3} ML^2 = \tfrac{1}{3} \times 2 \times 1^2 = \tfrac{2}{3} \text{ kg·m}^2 \]
\[ I = \tfrac{1}{12} \times 2 \times 1^2 = \tfrac{1}{6} \text{ kg·m}^2 \]

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Q1. A system consists of two identical spheres each of mass 1.5 kg and radius 50 cm at the end of a light rod. The distance between the centres of the two spheres is 5 m. What will be the moment of inertia of the system about an axis perpendicular to the rod passing through its mid-point? [JEE, 2021]

Answer: \( \boxed{\text{c) 19.05 kg\cdot m^2}} \)
