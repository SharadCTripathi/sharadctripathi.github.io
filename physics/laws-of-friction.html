Static and Kinetic Friction and Laws of Friction

Dr. Sharad Chandra Tripathi

What Is Friction?

Friction is a fundamental force encountered in everyday life and physics. It is a contact force that resists the relative motion or the tendency of motion between two surfaces in contact. Unlike forces such as gravity or tension, friction acts tangentially along the surfaces and always opposes the direction of potential or actual sliding.

Friction plays a crucial role in many practical situations, such as walking without slipping, car tires gripping the road, and the ability to hold objects.

Types of Friction

There are two primary types of friction:

  • Static Friction: This force acts when the object is at rest relative to the surface. It prevents the object from starting to move. Static friction adjusts itself to match the applied force up to a maximum limit. For instance, when you try to push a heavy box that doesn't move immediately, static friction is at work.
  • Kinetic (Sliding) Friction: Once the object begins to move, static friction transitions to kinetic friction, which acts against the motion during sliding. Kinetic friction typically has a smaller magnitude than the maximum static friction, making it easier to keep an object moving than to start it moving.

Laws of Friction

Five empirical laws govern friction:

  1. Frictional Force is Proportional to the Normal Force: The frictional force \( f \) depends directly on the normal force \( N \) (the perpendicular force between the surfaces). For static friction:
    2. \[ f \leq \mu_s N \]

    and for kinetic friction:

    \[ f_k = \mu_k N \]

    where \( \mu_s \) and \( \mu_k \) are the coefficients of static and kinetic friction, respectively.

  2. Static Friction Adjusts Up to a Maximum: Static friction is not fixed but can vary up to its maximum value \( \mu_s N \). If the applied force exceeds this, the object moves.
  3. Kinetic Friction is Constant and Less Than Static Friction:
    4. \[ \mu_k < \mu_s \]
  4. Friction is Independent of Contact Area: For dry friction (no lubrication), frictional force does not depend on the apparent area of contact. This is counterintuitive but explained by microscopic contact points.
  5. Friction Depends on Surface Nature: The types of materials and surface roughness directly affect the coefficients \( \mu_s \) and \( \mu_k \).

Key Concepts and Definitions

  • Normal Force (N): The perpendicular force exerted by a surface on an object resting on it. It balances the component of weight perpendicular to the surface.
  • Coefficient of Static Friction (\( \mu_s \)): The ratio of the maximum static friction force to the normal force. It represents how “sticky” the surfaces are at rest.
  • Coefficient of Kinetic Friction (\( \mu_k \)): The ratio of kinetic friction force to the normal force, representing resistance during motion.
  • Limiting Friction: The maximum possible static friction just before the object starts moving.
  • Frictional Force Direction: Always opposes the relative motion or the tendency of motion between surfaces, not necessarily opposing the applied force.

Understanding Friction Through Equations

Static friction obeys:

\[ f \leq \mu_s N \]

meaning the frictional force can increase as the applied force increases until it reaches \( \mu_s N \). Beyond this, motion begins.

Kinetic friction is constant during motion:

\[ f_k = \mu_k N \]

This explains why pushing a stationary object requires more force than keeping it sliding.

Shortcut Concepts for Quick Problem Solving

  • Use the inequality \( f \leq \mu_s N \) to determine if an object will move or remain at rest.
  • For motion on inclined planes, analyze the balance of forces along the plane:
    • \[ mg \sin \theta = \mu_s mg \cos \theta \quad \Rightarrow \quad \tan \theta = \mu_s \]

    where \( \theta \) is the angle of inclination.

  • Friction force opposes motion, not the applied force necessarily. If the applied force tries to move the object forward, friction acts backward; if the object moves backward, friction acts forward.
  • Rougher surfaces generally have larger coefficients of friction.

Practical Examples

  1. Block on Horizontal Surface

    Consider a block of mass 10 kg resting on a table. The coefficient of static friction \( \mu_s = 0.4 \). Find the minimum force needed to start moving the block.

    \[ N = mg = 10 \times 9.8 = 98\,\text{N} \]
    \[ f_{\text{max}} = \mu_s N = 0.4 \times 98 = 39.2\,\text{N} \]

    Thus, the minimum applied force to overcome static friction and initiate motion is \( \boxed{39.2\,\text{N}} \).

  2. Block on an Inclined Plane

    A block begins to slide down a plane inclined at \( 30^\circ \). Find \( \mu_s \).

    Using the relation:

    \[ \tan \theta = \mu_s \]
    \[ \mu_s = \tan 30^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \approx \boxed{0.577} \]

Why Does Friction Occur?

Friction arises due to microscopic irregularities and asperities on the surfaces, which interlock and resist motion. Additionally, intermolecular forces between surface atoms contribute. Even seemingly smooth surfaces have roughness at the microscopic scale.

Common Misconceptions About Friction

  • Friction Opposes Motion, Not Force: Friction always opposes relative motion or the potential for motion, not necessarily the applied force's direction.
  • Static Friction is Not Always Maximum: Static friction varies up to a maximum value depending on the applied force.
  • Kinetic Friction is Less Than Static: It’s easier to keep something moving than to start moving it because surfaces do not interlock as much once sliding.
  • Surface Area Doesn't Affect Dry Friction: Despite intuition, friction does not depend on the contact area but on the normal force and surface roughness.

Additional Insights on Friction

  • Rolling Friction: Different from sliding friction, rolling friction occurs when an object rolls over a surface. It is usually much smaller than sliding friction.
  • Lubricated Surfaces: Lubricants reduce friction by introducing a layer between surfaces, changing the interaction from dry friction to fluid friction.
  • Friction and Heat: Friction converts mechanical energy into heat energy, which is why moving parts can get hot.
  • Friction in Machines: Excessive friction causes wear and energy loss. Engineers aim to minimize friction in engines, bearings, and gears to improve efficiency.

Strategies for Solving Friction Problems Quickly

  • Draw Free-Body Diagrams (FBD): Identify all forces, especially normal and frictional forces.
  • Check Motion Conditions: Use \( f_{\text{max}} = \mu_s N \) to determine if motion starts or if the object stays at rest.
  • Analyze Inclined Planes Carefully: Resolve forces along and perpendicular to the plane.
  • Remember Direction of Friction: It always acts opposite to motion or impending motion.

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Q1.

A block of mass \( 100\,\text{kg} \) slides over a distance of \( 10\,\text{m} \) on a horizontal surface. If the coefficient of friction between the surfaces is \( 0.4 \), then the work done against friction (in J) is: (JEE 2024)

Options:

  1. \( 3900\,\text{J} \)
  2. \( 4500\,\text{J} \)
  3. \( 4200\,\text{J} \)
  4. \( 4000\,\text{J} \)

Solution:

  • Given:
    • \[ m = 100\,\text{kg}, \quad d = 10\,\text{m}, \quad \mu = 0.4, \quad g = 10\,\text{m/s}^2 \]
  • Normal force on horizontal surface:
    • \[ N = mg = 100 \cdot 10 = 1000\,\text{N} \]
  • Frictional force:
    • \[ f = \mu N = 0.4 \cdot 1000 = 400\,\text{N} \]
  • Work done against friction:
    • \[ W = f \cdot d = 400 \cdot 10 = \boxed{4000\,\text{J}} \]

Answer: \( \boxed{4000\,\text{J}} \)
