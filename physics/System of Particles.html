System of Particles and Centre of Mass

Dr. Sharad Chandra Tripathi

What Is the Centre of Mass of a System of Particles?

In physics, a system of particles consists of multiple bodies that may interact with each other and with external forces. To describe the motion of such a system in a simplified way, we use the concept of the Centre of Mass (COM). This point behaves as if the entire mass of the system were concentrated there.

Instead of tracking each particle’s individual motion, we often study the motion of the COM. This is particularly helpful in analyzing translational motion, collisions, explosions.

The concept of COM is not limited to rigid bodies or simple masses, it extends to systems with variable mass distributions and continuous bodies like rods, discs, or even irregularly shaped objects. In rotational dynamics, the axis of rotation often passes through the COM, because it minimizes the net torque required.

The COM is particularly important in analyzing mechanical systems, orbital motion of celestial bodies, and structural stability in engineering. In fact, walking, running, or even balancing are daily examples of COM application in biomechanics.

Key Concepts and Definitions

Let’s break down the key ideas related to systems of particles and their centre of mass:

  • Centre of Mass in 1D: \[x_{\text{COM}} = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2 + \cdots + m_nx_n}{m_1 + m_2 + \cdots + m_n}\] It’s the weighted average of positions based on mass. This expression can also be extended to two and three dimensions using the y and z coordinates.

  • Motion of COM: If no external force acts, the velocity of the COM remains constant. Even if individual particles move internally, the overall COM of the system follows Newton’s laws as if it were a single particle.

  • Shifting of COM: If a particle in the system moves, the COM shifts, unless another particle moves in such a way that the net COM position remains unchanged. This concept is essential in systems where internal rearrangements happen, like rockets ejecting fuel.

  • COM of Two Particles: For masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) at positions \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), \[x_{\text{COM}} = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2}{m_1 + m_2}\] The COM lies closer to the heavier mass. If both masses are equal, the COM lies exactly midway.

  • Conservation of Momentum: If no net external force acts on the system, \[\vec{P}_{\text{total}} = M\vec{v}_{\text{COM}} = \text{constant}\] This is a cornerstone of all COM-based motion analysis.

Shortcut Concepts

Here are some quick takeaways to solve COM problems efficiently:

  • To keep COM unchanged: movement of one mass must be balanced by movement of another.

  • Heavier masses need to move smaller distances to balance lighter masses moving longer distances.

  • Internal forces don’t affect the motion of the COM.

  • Always apply conservation of momentum for isolated systems.

  • COM motion is independent of internal energy transformations within the system.

Examples

Let’s reinforce our understanding with some basic examples:

  1. COM of two particles: A 2 kg mass is at 0 cm, and a 4 kg mass is at 6 cm. \[x_{\text{COM}} = \frac{2 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 6}{2 + 4} = \frac{24}{6} = 4\,\text{cm}\] So the COM lies 4 cm from the origin, closer to the heavier particle.

  2. COM of uniform rod: A uniform rod of length 2 m has its COM at its midpoint: 1 m. This holds true regardless of orientation or placement.

  3. Effect of movement on COM: If a 1 kg block at 0 cm moves to 4 cm, and a 3 kg block at 6 cm moves to \(x\), find \(x\) such that COM remains unchanged. \[\text{Initial COM} = \frac{1 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 6}{4} = 4.5\,\text{cm}\] \[\text{Final COM} = \frac{1 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot x}{4} = 4.5 \Rightarrow x = 4.5\]

Concept Questions with Explanations

  1. Is COM always within the body?
    No. For hollow or oddly shaped objects, the COM can lie outside the material. A ring or a boomerang are classic examples.

  2. Does gravity act at the COM?
    Yes, the net gravitational force on a body acts at its COM. That’s why hanging an object from its COM allows it to balance perfectly.

  3. Can COM move if no external force acts?
    No. In an isolated system, the COM either remains at rest or moves at constant velocity. Internal movements can’t change COM’s velocity.

  4. Do internal forces shift COM?
    No. Internal forces, even if they rearrange particles, do not affect the COM’s motion.

  5. What affects the COM location?
    Mass distribution and particle positions determine the COM. Adding mass or shifting particles will change its location accordingly.

Super Tips for Solving Fast

To solve problems involving COM:

  • Use weighted average formula for COM in 1D, 2D, or 3D.

  • Treat each motion individually, and apply the condition: COM unchanged \(\Rightarrow\) total weighted position must remain the same.

  • For symmetrical bodies, COM lies at the geometric center.

  • Always keep track of direction—signs matter!

  • Use relative motion to simplify problems where objects move toward or away from each other.

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Q1. Two blocks of masses 10 kg and 30 kg are placed on the same straight line with coordinates (0,0) cm and (x,0) cm, respectively. The block of 10 kg is moved on the same line through a distance of 6 cm towards the other block. The distance through which the block of 30 kg must be moved to keep the position of centre of mass of the system unchanged is:
[JEE, 2022]

  1. 4 cm towards the 10 kg block

  2. 2 cm away from the 10 kg block

  3. 2 cm towards the 10 kg block

  4. 4 cm away from the 10 kg block

Solution:

Let the initial positions be: \[x_1 = 0\,\text{cm}, \quad x_2 = x\,\text{cm}\]

Initial COM: \[x_{\text{COM}} = \frac{10 \cdot 0 + 30 \cdot x}{40} = \frac{3x}{4}\]

After moving: \[x_1' = 6\,\text{cm (towards 30 kg block)}, \quad x_2' = x + d\]

New COM: \[x'_{\text{COM}} = \frac{10 \cdot 6 + 30(x + d)}{40} = \frac{60 + 30x + 30d}{40}\]

Equating both COMs: \[\frac{3x}{4} = \frac{60 + 30x + 30d}{40} \Rightarrow 30x = 60 + 30x + 30d \Rightarrow 0 = 60 + 30d \Rightarrow d = -2\,\text{cm}\]

So the 30 kg block must move 2 cm **towards** the 10 kg block.

Answer: \(\boxed{\text{c) 2 cm towards the 10 kg block}}\)
