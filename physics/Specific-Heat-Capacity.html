Specific Heat Capacity

Why does water take longer to heat than metal? Why does sand get hot quickly on a sunny day but cools down just as fast at night? The answer lies in a property called specific heat capacity. It tells us how much heat is required to raise the temperature of a substance and helps explain the thermal behavior of everything from oceans to engine blocks.

Let us understand this concept in depth.

What is Specific Heat Capacity?

Specific heat capacity is defined as the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of one gram of a substance by one degree Celsius (or one kelvin).

Mathematically, it is expressed as:

\[Q = mc\Delta T\]

Where:

  • \(Q\) = heat supplied (in joules)

  • \(m\) = mass of the substance (in grams or kilograms)

  • \(c\) = specific heat capacity of the substance

  • \(\Delta T\) = change in temperature

SI unit: \(\text{J/kg} \cdot \text{K}\) CGS unit: \(\text{cal/g}^\circ \text{C}\)

Note: 1 calorie = 4.186 joules

Heat Capacity vs Specific Heat Capacity

Heat Capacity (C): It is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of the entire object by 1°C. It depends on mass and material.

\[C = mc\]

So, specific heat capacity is the heat capacity per unit mass.

Water Has High Specific Heat

Water has a very high specific heat capacity, around \(4186 \, \text{J/kg} \cdot \text{K}\). This is why:

  • Oceans regulate Earth’s temperature.

  • Water-based coolants are used in engines.

  • Human body (mostly water) resists sudden temperature changes.

Understanding Through an Example

Suppose you supply 4200 J of heat to 1 kg of water. The temperature of water will rise by:

\[\Delta T = \frac{Q}{mc} = \frac{4200}{1 \times 4200} = 1^\circ \text{C}\]

But if you supplied the same heat to copper (with a much lower \(c\)), the temperature rise would be much more.

Molar Specific Heat

Instead of per gram or kilogram, sometimes heat capacity is given per mole:

\[C_{\text{molar}} = \frac{Q}{n \Delta T}\]

Where \(n\) is the number of moles. Molar specific heat is often used in thermodynamics, especially for gases.

Types of Specific Heat

  • At constant volume (\(C_V\)): Heat required at constant volume.

  • At constant pressure (\(C_P\)): Heat required at constant pressure.

For gases,

\[C_P - C_V = R\]

Where \(R\) is the universal gas constant.

Calorimetry and Heat Exchange

Calorimetry is the study of measuring heat changes during physical or chemical processes. It is based on the principle of conservation of energy:

\[\text{Heat lost by hot body} = \text{Heat gained by cold body}\]

\[m_1 c_1 (T_1 - T_f) = m_2 c_2 (T_f - T_2)\]

Where:

  • \(T_1\), \(T_2\) are initial temperatures

  • \(T_f\) is final equilibrium temperature

Factors Affecting Temperature Rise

The amount of temperature increase due to heat depends on:

  • Mass: More mass means slower temperature rise.

  • Specific Heat: High \(c\) means more energy needed.

  • Energy supplied: More heat causes greater temperature change.

Role in Daily Life and Industry

  • Cooking utensils: Made of materials with low specific heat so they heat up quickly.

  • Thermal buffering: Water in hot-water bottles or heat pads stores energy longer.

  • Climate control: Coastal areas have moderate climates due to the high specific heat of water.

  • Metallurgy: Understanding temperature changes during heating and forging processes.

Mechanical Work and Heat

Mechanical work (like hammering) can convert kinetic energy into heat, raising the temperature of an object. For example, when a hammer hits a metal nail, part of the kinetic energy of the hammer is transferred as thermal energy to the nail.

Kinetic energy: \[E = \frac{1}{2} m v^2\]

If only a fraction of this energy is absorbed by the object, we can calculate the resulting temperature rise using:

\[Q = mc\Delta T\]

Note: The fraction of energy converted to heat depends on the efficiency of transfer, surface contact, and material properties.

Shortcut Concepts

  • Specific heat \(c\): Heat required per unit mass per unit rise in temperature.

  • Formula: \(Q = mc\Delta T\)

  • High \(c\) → heats up slowly; low \(c\) → heats up quickly.

  • Water has highest known specific heat among common substances.

  • \(C = mc\), heat capacity is the total heat needed.

  • Mechanical energy can convert to heat, causing temperature rise.

Quick Review Questions

  1. What is the unit of specific heat capacity in SI?
    \(\text{J/kg} \cdot \text{K}\)

  2. What is the specific heat of water?
    Approximately \(4186 \, \text{J/kg} \cdot \text{K}\)

  3. If you supply 1000 J to 500 g of copper (\(c = 0.385 \, \text{J/g°C}\)), what is the temperature rise?
    \[\Delta T = \frac{1000}{500 \times 0.385} \approx 5.19^\circ C\]

  4. Which heats faster: water or copper?
    Copper, because it has lower specific heat.

  5. What is the relation between \(C_P\) and \(C_V\) for an ideal gas?
    \(C_P - C_V = R\)

Previous Year Question (PYQ)

Q.1 A 100 g of iron nail is hit by a 1.5 kg hammer striking at a velocity of \(60 \, \text{ms}^{-1}\). What will be the rise in the temperature of the nail if one-fourth of the energy of the hammer goes into heating the nail? [Specific heat capacity of iron = \(0.42 \, \text{Jg}^{-1}{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\)] [JEE, 2022]

Options: a) \(675^\circ \text{C}\), b) \(1600^\circ \text{C}\), c) \(16.07^\circ \text{C}\), d) \(6.75^\circ \text{C}\)

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate total kinetic energy of hammer

\[E = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 1.5 \times (60)^2 = 0.75 \times 3600 = 2700 \, \text{J}\]

Step 2: Only one-fourth of energy heats the nail

\[Q = \frac{1}{4} \times 2700 = 675 \, \text{J}\]

Step 3: Use heat formula to find temperature rise

\[Q = mc\Delta T \Rightarrow \Delta T = \frac{Q}{mc} = \frac{675}{100 \times 0.42} = \frac{675}{42} \approx 16.07^\circ \text{C}\]

Answer: (c) \(16.07^\circ \text{C}\)
