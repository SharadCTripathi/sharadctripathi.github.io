Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

Dr. Sharad Chandra Tripathi

Introduction

In mechanics, forces can be broadly divided into two main types: conservative and non-conservative forces. Recognizing which type of force is acting helps us understand how energy behaves in a system—whether it stays within the system or gets transformed into other forms like heat or sound.

Conservative Forces

A conservative force is one where the work done in moving an object between two points doesn’t depend on the path taken. It only depends on where you start and where you finish.

  • Work Around a Loop: If an object is moved around a closed path and ends up where it started, the total work done by a conservative force is zero: \[W_{\text{closed}} = 0\]

  • Examples of Conservative Forces:

    • Gravity

    • Electrostatic force

    • Ideal spring (elastic force)

  • Associated with Potential Energy: Conservative forces can be linked to potential energy. Mathematically: \[\vec{F} = -\nabla U\] - For gravity near Earth’s surface: \[U = mgh\] - For a spring: \[U = \frac{1}{2} k x^2\]

  • Mechanical Energy is Conserved: If only conservative forces are doing work, the total mechanical energy of the system stays constant: \[\text{Total Energy} = \text{K.E.} + \text{P.E.} = \text{constant}\]

Non-Conservative Forces

Non-conservative forces are different. The work they do depends on the path taken, and they often convert mechanical energy into other forms like heat or sound.

  • Path-Dependent Work: The work done by non-conservative forces in a closed path is not zero: \[W_{\text{closed}} \neq 0\]

  • Examples of Non-Conservative Forces:

    • Friction

    • Air resistance (drag)

    • Viscous forces (like in liquids)

    • Tension in non-ideal ropes or strings

  • They Cause Energy Loss: These forces reduce the mechanical energy of a system, often turning it into heat: \[\text{Initial Mechanical Energy} > \text{Final Mechanical Energy}\]

  • No Potential Energy Function: You can’t describe non-conservative forces using potential energy equations.

Quick Comparison

Feature Conservative Force Non-Conservative Force
Work in a closed path Zero Non-zero
Path dependence Independent of path Dependent on path
Energy conservation Mechanical energy conserved Mechanical energy not conserved
Associated potential energy Yes No
Examples Gravity, Spring force, Electrostatic force Friction, Air resistance, Viscous drag

Example: Lifting a Block — Two Different Paths

Q: Suppose you’re lifting a block up by \(5\,\text{m}\) vertically, or pushing it up a ramp to the same height. Which forces care about the path taken?

Answer: Gravity doesn’t—it’s conservative and only depends on height. But friction, which acts along the ramp, definitely depends on the path taken and adds extra energy loss.

Work done by gravity: \[W = -mgh \quad \text{(same for both paths)}\]

Work done by friction (on ramp): \[W_{\text{friction}} = \mu mg \cos\theta \cdot \text{distance along ramp}\]

Key Takeaway: Gravity is a conservative force—it’s path-independent. Friction is non-conservative—it depends on the path and causes energy loss.

Helpful Tips to Remember

  • If you can define potential energy for a force, it’s conservative.

  • Friction always leads to mechanical energy loss—it’s always non-conservative.

  • Use energy conservation methods for gravity and springs.

  • If mechanical energy decreases in a system, a non-conservative force is likely acting.

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Q2. A particle is released from height \(S\) from the surface of the Earth. At a certain height, its kinetic energy is three times its potential energy. The height from the surface of the Earth and the speed of the particle at that instant are respectively -

  1. \(\frac{S}{4}, \sqrt{\frac{3gS}{2}}\)

  2. \(\frac{S}{4}, \frac{3gS}{2}\)

  3. \(\frac{S}{4}, \frac{\sqrt{3gS}}{2}\)

  4. \(\frac{S}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3gS}}{2}\)

NEET 2021

Solution:

Given: - Initial height = \(S\) - At some height \(h\), kinetic energy \(K = 3 \times\) potential energy \(U\).

Using conservation of mechanical energy:

\[mgS = mg h + \frac{1}{2} m v^2\]

Given:

\[K = 3U \implies \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = 3 m g h \implies v^2 = 6 g h\]

Substitute \(v^2 = 6 g h\) into the energy equation:

\[mgS = mg h + \frac{1}{2} m (6 g h) = mg h + 3 m g h = 4 m g h\]

Divide both sides by \(mg\):

\[S = 4 h \implies h = \frac{S}{4}\]

Now, find speed \(v\):

\[v = \sqrt{6 g h} = \sqrt{6 g \times \frac{S}{4}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 g S}{2}}\]

Answer: \[\boxed{ \text{Height} = \frac{S}{4}, \quad \text{Speed} = \sqrt{\frac{3 g S}{2}} }\]

So, option (a) is correct.
