Biot–Savart Law and the Magnetic Field of a Circular Current Loop

When electric charges move, something remarkable happens around them — they create a magnetic field. This connection between electricity and magnetism is one of the most fascinating discoveries in physics.

The Biot–Savart Law provides a precise mathematical way to calculate the magnetic field produced by a current-carrying conductor. In spirit, it plays a role similar to Coulomb’s law in electrostatics, but instead of charges producing electric fields, moving charges (currents) produce magnetic fields.

---

Magnetic Field Due to a Small Current Element

Imagine a tiny segment of a wire carrying current. Let the current be I and the small length of the wire be represented by the vector dℓ.

The magnetic field produced by this small current element at some observation point in space is given by the Biot–Savart law:

$$ d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\, d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2} $$

Here,

  • μ₀ – permeability of free space
  • I – current in the conductor
  • dℓ – infinitesimal current element
  • r – distance between the current element and the observation point
  • r̂ – unit vector pointing from the element toward the observation point

Notice the cross product in the expression. This tells us something important: the magnetic field is always perpendicular to both the current direction and the line joining the current element to the observation point.

---

Total Magnetic Field of a Conductor

A real conductor is not just a tiny element; it contains many such elements. To find the total magnetic field produced by the entire conductor, we simply add the contributions from all current elements.

$$ \vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int \frac{I\, d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2} $$

This integral forms the basis for calculating magnetic fields of wires, loops, coils, and many other current distributions.

---

Magnetic Field of a Long Straight Conductor

One of the most important applications of the Biot–Savart law is the magnetic field around a long straight current-carrying wire.

If a wire carries a steady current I, the magnetic field at a distance r from the wire is

$$ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} $$

The direction of the magnetic field is determined by the right-hand thumb rule.

Point the thumb of your right hand in the direction of current. The curl of your fingers shows the direction of the magnetic field lines around the wire.

---

Magnetic Field of a Circular Current Loop

Now consider a circular loop of radius R carrying a steady current I.

Because of symmetry, each small segment of the loop contributes to the magnetic field at the center in such a way that the horizontal components cancel out. Only the vertical components add together.

---

Magnetic Field at the Center

The magnetic field at the center of the loop becomes

$$ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R} $$

The direction of this field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop, again determined using the right-hand rule.

---

Magnetic Field Along the Axis of the Loop

At a point located a distance x along the axis of the loop, the magnetic field is

$$ B = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2 (R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} $$

When x = 0, the expression naturally reduces to the magnetic field at the center of the loop.

---

Important Observations

  • Because of symmetry, only the axial component of the magnetic field survives.
  • The magnetic field decreases rapidly as we move away from the loop.
  • The direction of the field is always given by the right-hand rule.
---

Worked Example

Suppose a circular loop of radius 0.05 m carries a current of 5 A. What is the magnetic field at the center?

$$ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R} $$ Substituting values: $$ B = \frac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 5}{2 \times 0.05} $$ $$ B = 10^{-5}\pi \, T $$ ---

---

Quick Takeaways

  • Magnetic fields arise due to moving charges.
  • The Biot–Savart law gives the magnetic field due to a small current element.
  • The magnetic field at the center of a loop is μ₀I / 2R.
  • The field around a straight conductor is μ₀I / 2πr.
