Biot–Savart Law

Whenever electric charges move through a conductor, they produce a magnetic field around them. Understanding how this magnetic field is generated is one of the key ideas in electromagnetism. The Biot–Savart Law gives us a mathematical relationship that allows us to calculate the magnetic field produced by a current element.

If you have studied Coulomb’s law in electrostatics, the Biot–Savart law plays a similar role in magnetism. Instead of stationary charges producing electric fields, moving charges (currents) produce magnetic fields.

Magnetic Field Due to a Current Element

Consider a very small segment of a current-carrying wire represented by the vector $d\vec{l}$. If the current flowing through the conductor is $I$, the magnetic field produced at a point located a distance $r$ away is given by the Biot–Savart law.

$$ d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\, d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2} $$

In this expression:

  • $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space
  • $I$ represents the current flowing in the conductor
  • $d\vec{l}$ is the infinitesimal length element of the conductor
  • $r$ is the distance between the current element and the observation point
  • $\hat{r}$ is the unit vector pointing from the element toward the observation point

Notice the cross product in the formula. This tells us that the magnetic field is always perpendicular to both the current direction and the line joining the element to the observation point.

Total Magnetic Field of a Conductor

A real wire consists of many such small current elements. To determine the total magnetic field produced by the entire conductor, we simply add the contribution of every element along the wire.

$$ \vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int \frac{I\, d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2} $$

This integral expression forms the foundation for calculating magnetic fields of different current configurations such as straight wires, loops, coils and solenoids.

Magnetic Field Around a Long Straight Conductor

One of the simplest and most useful results derived from the Biot–Savart law is the magnetic field around a long straight current-carrying wire.

If a wire carries current $I$, the magnetic field at a distance $r$ from the wire is

$$ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} $$

The direction of the magnetic field can be easily determined using the right-hand thumb rule.

If you point the thumb of your right hand along the direction of current, the curl of your fingers gives the direction of the magnetic field lines surrounding the conductor.

Magnetic Field of a Circular Current Loop

Now imagine bending the wire into a circular loop of radius $R$ and passing a current $I$ through it. Every small segment of the loop contributes to the magnetic field at the center.

Because of symmetry, the horizontal components cancel out, leaving only the vertical component.

Magnetic Field at the Center

$$ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R} $$

The direction of the magnetic field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop and is again determined using the right-hand rule.

Magnetic Field on the Axis of the Loop

At a point located a distance $x$ from the center along the axis of the loop, the magnetic field becomes

$$ B = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} $$

If we substitute $x = 0$, the expression naturally reduces to the magnetic field at the center.

Worked Example

Suppose a circular loop of radius $0.05\,m$ carries a current of $5\,A$. The magnetic field at the center of the loop is given by

$$ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R} $$ Substituting the values, $$ B = \frac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 5}{2 \times 0.05} $$ $$ B = 10^{-5}\pi \,T $$

Key Takeaways

  • The Biot–Savart law relates electric current to the magnetic field it produces.
  • The magnetic field around a straight conductor decreases inversely with distance.
  • A circular loop produces a magnetic field perpendicular to its plane.
  • Symmetry greatly simplifies magnetic field calculations.
  • The right-hand rule helps determine magnetic field direction quickly.
