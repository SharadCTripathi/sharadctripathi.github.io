Moment of Inertia

Imagine you are trying to spin a big wheel. Sometimes it is easy to spin, and sometimes it is hard. Why does this happen?

This is because of something called moment of inertia. It helps us understand how much an object resists spinning.

Moment of inertia is like “mass” but for spinning things. Just like heavy things are hard to move, things with a big moment of inertia are hard to spin or stop spinning.

Here are some important points to remember:

  • Moment of inertia tells us how hard it is to start or stop spinning.

  • It depends on two things: how heavy the object is, and how far the weight is from the center of spinning (called the axis).

  • If the weight is far from the axis, the object is harder to spin.

  • If the weight is close to the axis, the object is easier to spin.

Think about opening a door. If you push near the hinge (the axis), it is hard to open. But if you push at the edge of the door (far from the hinge), it opens easily. This happens because pushing far from the axis gives more turning power.

In the same way, objects with weight far from their center resist spinning more. This means they have a bigger moment of inertia.

So, moment of inertia is how much something resists spinning. It depends on how heavy it is and where its weight is placed.

How Is Moment of Inertia Calculated?

Let’s break it down clearly:

  • For a single point mass: \[I = m r^2\] where \(m\) is the mass and \(r\) is the distance from the axis of rotation.

  • For an extended object, split it into tiny mass elements and sum them: \[I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2\] or, in the continuous case using calculus, \[I = \int r^2 \, dm\]

This integral adds up all the tiny contributions to the total resistance to spinning.

Why Should You Care?

Moment of inertia plays a role in many everyday and technical situations:

  • Engine flywheels: These help maintain smooth engine performance by resisting sudden changes in rotational speed. Their large moment of inertia stores rotational energy and prevents stalling.

  • Figure skaters: When skaters pull in their arms, they spin faster. This is because reducing their moment of inertia increases angular velocity to conserve angular momentum.

  • Bicycle wheels: Larger wheels have more moment of inertia, helping maintain forward motion. That’s why once you’re rolling, staying upright becomes easier.

  • Industrial machines: From turbines to washing machines, engineers adjust the moment of inertia to control torque, acceleration, and energy efficiency.

It’s also a key player in Newton’s second law for rotation: \[\tau = I \alpha\] where \(\tau\) is torque, \(I\) is moment of inertia, and \(\alpha\) is angular acceleration. Just like \(F = ma\) governs linear motion, this governs how rotational speed changes under applied torque.

Common Shapes and Their Moments of Inertia

Here are some classic shapes and their formulas:

  • Thin ring about its central axis: \[I = MR^2\]

  • Disc or solid cylinder through center: \[I = \tfrac{1}{2} MR^2\]

  • Thin rod:

    • About center: \(I = \tfrac{1}{12} ML^2\)

    • About one end: \(I = \tfrac{1}{3} ML^2\)

  • Solid sphere through its diameter: \[I = \tfrac{2}{5} MR^2\]

  • Hollow sphere through its diameter: \[I = \tfrac{2}{3} MR^2\]

Knowing these helps solve problems quickly without re-deriving each time.

Useful Theorems

Two theorems make calculating moment of inertia more flexible:

  • Parallel Axis Theorem If you know the moment of inertia about an object’s center of mass, and you want it about another parallel axis: \[I = I_{\text{CM}} + M d^2\] where \(d\) is the distance between axes. Useful for rods, beams, and offset parts.

  • Perpendicular Axis Theorem (for flat shapes): For objects like discs or plates lying in the XY-plane: \[I_z = I_x + I_y\] where \(I_z\) is the moment about an axis perpendicular to the plane. A quick trick when symmetry helps.

These are invaluable when designing stable structures, rotating components, or analyzing torque effects in robotics and aerospace.

Worked Example

Let’s say you have a uniform rod, 1 meter long and 2 kg, rotating about one end:

\[I = \tfrac{1}{3} ML^2 = \tfrac{1}{3} \times 2 \times 1^2 = \tfrac{2}{3} \text{ kg·m}^2\]

Now, if the same rod spins about its center:

\[I = \tfrac{1}{12} \times 2 \times 1^2 = \tfrac{1}{6} \text{ kg·m}^2\]

Notice the difference: when rotating about the end, more mass is farther from the axis, increasing the moment of inertia. This means more torque is needed to spin it with the same acceleration.

This example shows how simply changing the axis changes the dynamics significantly. It also explains why tools like baseball bats feel heavier when held from the end—your hands act as the rotation axis.

Key Insights to Remember

Moment of inertia is a core idea in rotational mechanics, and understanding it gives you deeper control over how objects behave when they spin. Instead of just memorizing formulas, grasping how mass distribution affects rotation allows you to predict and explain real-world motion confidently.

Here are the main insights to take forward:

  • Mass positioned farther from the axis increases the moment of inertia and makes rotation more difficult.

  • A larger moment of inertia always means more resistance to changes in rotational motion, it’s the rotational equivalent of “heaviness.”

  • Torque and moment of inertia work together to determine angular acceleration, following the relation \(\tau = I\alpha\).

  • Remembering standard formulas for common shapes saves time and makes solving physics problems more efficient.

  • Real-life systems, from sports and vehicles to machinery and engineering designs, rely on a proper understanding of how mass is distributed.

  • Visual examples like doors, skaters, and spinning wheels help make the concept more intuitive and relatable.

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Q1. A system consists of two identical spheres each of mass 1.5 kg and radius 50 cm at the end of a light rod. The distance between the centres of the two spheres is 5 m. What will be the moment of inertia of the system about an axis perpendicular to the rod passing through its mid-point?
[JEE, 2021]

  1. 18.75 kg\(\cdot\)m\(^2\)

  2. 1.905 \(\times\) 10\(^5\) kg\(\cdot\)m\(^2\)

  3. 19.05 kg\(\cdot\)m\(^2\)

  4. 1.875 \(\times\) 10\(^5\) kg\(\cdot\)m\(^2\)

Solution:

Each sphere contributes to the moment of inertia in two parts:

  • Its own moment of inertia about its center: \(I_{\text{sphere}} = \frac{2}{5}MR^2\)

  • Using the parallel axis theorem, shift to the axis through the midpoint of the rod: \[I_{\text{total}} = \frac{2}{5}MR^2 + M\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2\]

Given: \[M = 1.5\,\text{kg}, \quad R = 0.5\,\text{m}, \quad d = 5\,\text{m}\]

For each sphere: \[I = \frac{2}{5}(1.5)(0.5)^2 + 1.5\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{2}{5}(1.5)(0.25) + 1.5(6.25) = 0.15 + 9.375 = 9.525\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\]

Since there are two identical spheres: \[I_{\text{total}} = 2 \times 9.525 = 19.05\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\]

Answer: \(\boxed{\text{c) 19.05 kg$\cdot$m$^2$}}\)
