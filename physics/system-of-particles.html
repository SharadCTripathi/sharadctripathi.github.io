What Is the Centre of Mass of a System of Particles?

In physics, a system of particles consists of multiple bodies that may interact with each other and with external forces. To describe the motion of such a system in a simplified way, we use the concept of the Centre of Mass (COM). This point behaves as if the entire mass of the system were concentrated there.

Instead of tracking each particle’s individual motion, we often study the motion of the COM. This is particularly helpful in analyzing translational motion, collisions, explosions, or even complex events like multi-stage rockets. By focusing on the COM, we can predict the overall motion without being bogged down by internal vibrations or rotations.

The concept of COM is not limited to rigid bodies or simple masses, it extends to systems with variable mass distributions and continuous bodies like rods, discs, or even irregularly shaped objects. In rotational dynamics, the axis of rotation often passes through the COM, because it minimizes the net torque required. Interestingly, for non-uniform bodies, the COM can lie outside the physical material, leading to counterintuitive behaviors, such as the balancing of a boomerang in mid-air.

The COM is particularly important in analyzing mechanical systems, orbital motion of celestial bodies, and structural stability in engineering. In fact, walking, running, or even balancing are daily examples of COM application in biomechanics. In spacecraft dynamics, the COM determines the trajectory during free flight, while in sports, athletes adjust body posture to control their COM for jumps, flips, or turns.

Key Concepts and Definitions

Let’s break down the key ideas related to systems of particles and their centre of mass:

  • Centre of Mass in 1D:
    \[ x_{\text{COM}} = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2 + \cdots + m_nx_n}{m_1 + m_2 + \cdots + m_n} \]
    It’s the weighted average of positions based on mass. This expression can also be extended to two and three dimensions using the y and z coordinates. Conceptually, it’s like “balancing” a seesaw: the heavier masses pull the COM closer to them.
  • Motion of COM: If no external force acts, the velocity of the COM remains constant. Even if individual particles move internally, the overall COM of the system follows Newton’s laws as if it were a single particle. This principle is foundational in analyzing rockets, where internal fuel ejection doesn’t accelerate the COM beyond the net external forces.
  • Shifting of COM: If a particle in the system moves, the COM shifts, unless another particle moves in such a way that the net COM position remains unchanged. This concept is essential in systems where internal rearrangements happen, like rockets ejecting fuel, athletes shifting body mass to stabilize balance, or ships adjusting ballast.
  • COM of Two Particles: For masses \( m_1 \) and \( m_2 \) at positions \( x_1 \) and \( x_2 \),
    \[ x_{\text{COM}} = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2}{m_1 + m_2} \]
    The COM lies closer to the heavier mass. If both masses are equal, the COM lies exactly midway. This is the simplest illustration of how mass distribution influences the center of gravity.
  • Conservation of Momentum: If no net external force acts on the system,
    \[ \vec{P}_{\text{total}} = M\vec{v}_{\text{COM}} = \text{constant} \]
    This is a cornerstone of all COM-based motion analysis. It also underpins collision analysis in both elastic and inelastic cases, showing why the system’s COM follows a predictable path regardless of internal exchanges of momentum.

Shortcut Concepts

  • To keep COM unchanged: movement of one mass must be balanced by movement of another.
  • Heavier masses need to move smaller distances to balance lighter masses moving longer distances, reflecting the inverse proportionality between mass and displacement in COM calculations.
  • Internal forces don’t affect the motion of the COM. This is why tension in strings, springs, or inter-particle forces cannot move the COM in isolation.
  • Always apply conservation of momentum for isolated systems—this is especially powerful in multi-body collisions, rocket propulsion, and astrophysical systems like binary stars.
  • COM motion is independent of internal energy transformations within the system. Whether particles vibrate, rotate, or exchange kinetic energy, the COM trajectory remains governed solely by external forces.

Examples

  1. COM of two particles: A 2 kg mass is at 0 cm, and a 4 kg mass is at 6 cm.
    \[ x_{\text{COM}} = \frac{2 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 6}{2 + 4} = \frac{24}{6} = 4\,\text{cm} \]
    So the COM lies 4 cm from the origin, closer to the heavier particle. This shows the intuitive “mass-weighted pull” effect.
  2. COM of uniform rod: A uniform rod of length 2 m has its COM at its midpoint: 1 m. This holds true regardless of orientation or placement. In physics, this is why a uniform seesaw balances perfectly at the center.
  3. Effect of movement on COM: If a 1 kg block at 0 cm moves to 4 cm, and a 3 kg block at 6 cm moves to \( x \), find \( x \) such that COM remains unchanged.
    \[ \text{Initial COM} = \frac{1 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 6}{4} = 4.5\,\text{cm} \]
    \[ \text{Final COM} = \frac{1 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot x}{4} = 4.5 \Rightarrow x = 4.5 \]
    This example demonstrates the practical application of “balanced movement” to maintain COM, a principle used in robotics and biomechanics for stable motion.

Concept Questions with Explanations

  1. Is COM always within the body?
    No. For hollow or oddly shaped objects, the COM can lie outside the material. A ring or a boomerang are classic examples. This is a subtle but important point in understanding torque and rotational stability.
  2. Does gravity act at the COM?
    Yes, the net gravitational force on a body acts at its COM. That’s why hanging an object from its COM allows it to balance perfectly. Engineers exploit this principle in cranes, bridges, and pendulums.
  3. Can COM move if no external force acts?
    No. In an isolated system, the COM either remains at rest or moves at constant velocity. Internal movements can’t change COM’s velocity—a principle crucial in space flight, where astronauts can move within a spacecraft without altering the trajectory of the COM.
  4. Do internal forces shift COM?
    No. Internal forces, even if they rearrange particles, do not affect the COM’s motion. This is why rocket fuel ejection changes the rocket’s velocity (an external action) but not the COM of the combined rocket-fuel system in free space.
  5. What affects the COM location?
    Mass distribution and particle positions determine the COM. Adding mass or shifting particles will change its location accordingly. This is used in designing vehicles, aircraft, and athletic strategies to control balance and stability.

Super Tips for Solving Fast

  • Use weighted average formula for COM in 1D, 2D, or 3D. Think of it as the “balance point” along all axes.
  • Treat each motion individually, and apply the condition: COM unchanged \( \Rightarrow \) total weighted position must remain the same. This is especially helpful in multi-particle systems or when dealing with variable masses.
  • For symmetrical bodies, COM lies at the geometric center, but always verify in case of non-uniform density.
  • Always keep track of direction—signs matter! Positive and negative coordinates can drastically change COM calculations.
  • Use relative motion to simplify problems where objects move toward or away from each other. This often transforms complex multi-body problems into a single-body COM problem.
  • Remember subtle physics: for continuous bodies, integrate over mass elements to find COM, and for rotating systems, COM often dictates torque and angular momentum relationships.

Previous Year Questions (PYQs)

Q1.
Two blocks of masses 10 kg and 30 kg are placed on the same straight line with coordinates (0,0) cm and (x,0) cm, respectively. The block of 10 kg is moved on the same line through a distance of 6 cm towards the other block. The distance through which the block of 30 kg must be moved to keep the position of centre of mass of the system unchanged is:
[JEE, 2022]

(a) 4 cm towards the 10 kg block
(b) 2 cm away from the 10 kg block
(c) 2 cm towards the 10 kg block
(d) 4 cm away from the 10 kg block

Solution:

Let the initial positions be:

\[ x_1 = 0\,\text{cm}, \quad x_2 = x\,\text{cm} \]

Initial COM:

\[ x_{\text{COM}} = \frac{10 \cdot 0 + 30 \cdot x}{40} = \frac{3x}{4} \]

After moving:

\[ x_1' = 6\,\text{cm (towards 30 kg block)}, \quad x_2' = x + d \]

New COM:

\[ x'_{\text{COM}} = \frac{10 \cdot 6 + 30(x + d)}{40} = \frac{60 + 30x + 30d}{40} \]

Equating both COMs:

\[ \frac{3x}{4} = \frac{60 + 30x + 30d}{40} \Rightarrow 30x = 60 + 30x + 30d \Rightarrow 0 = 60 + 30d \Rightarrow d = -2\,\text{cm} \]

So the 30 kg block must move 2 cm towards the 10 kg block. Notice how the negative sign indicates the direction is opposite to the positive axis—tracking direction is key.

Answer: \( \boxed{\text{c) 2 cm towards the 10 kg block}} \)
